I pursued a bachelor’s degree in Physics from the University of Colorado (CU) in Boulder. During my undergraduate studies, I conducted research projects in plasma physics, planetary science, and geomechanics. I graduated magna cum laude with a research project developing and testing a model that simulated dust rings around the centaur Chariklo at the <a href="https://impact.colorado.edu/">Institute for Modeling Plasma, Atmosphere and Cosmic Dust (IMPACT)</a>. The transition towards atmospheric science developed naturally when being introduced to the chaotic nature of weather patterns in classical mechanics, nonlinear dynamics, and geomechanics courses. My love of remote sensing came about through my Research Experience for Undergraduates (REU) position at the <a href="https://www.haystack.mit.edu/"> MIT Haystack Observatory</a>, where I combined precise, real-time measurements from GPS with measurements over large spatial regions from NASA JPL’s Uninhabited Aerial Vehicle Synthetic Aperture Radar (UAVSAR) data to study Earth surface deformations. 

I received an M.S. (2019) and Ph.D (2023) working in <a href="https://lecuyer.aos.wisc.edu/">Dr. Tristan L'Ecuyer's research group</a> in the <a href="https://www.aos.wisc.edu/">Department of Atmospheric and Oceanic Sciences (AOS)</a> at the University of Wisconsin (UW), Madison. During my graduate studies, I analyzed convective storm system properties to understand their present-day contributions to the Earth’s energy budget and water cycle. Twice-daily measurements from the A-Train satellite constellation were used to capture cloud, precipitation, and radiative effects of convective storm systems on a cloud object scale, and were supplemented with geostationary satellite observations and reanalyses to provide horizontal and temporal context to the storm features in differing environments.

From 2024 to 2025, I worked as a postdoctoral research scientist with Dr. Gregory Cesana at the <a href="https://ccsr.columbia.edu/">Center for Climate Systems Research (CCSR)</a> at Columbia University with a joint affiliation through <a href="https://www.giss.nasa.gov/">NASA Goddard Institute for Space Studies (GISS)</a>. My research used satellite observations to analyze high cloud feedbacks to better constrain climate model output. I recently began a postdoctoral fellowship in <a href="https://vandenheever.atmos.colostate.edu/vdhpage/vdh.php">Dr. Sue van den Heever's group</a> in the <a href="https://www.atmos.colostate.edu/">Department of Atmospheric Science</a> at Colorado State University. My postdoc research at CSU builds upon my PhD work and is motivated by the science goals of the <a href="https://incus.colostate.edu/">NASA INCUS mission</a>. I am currently analyzing and tracking deep convective systems using multiple satellite observations to characterise storm system morphologies, such as the link between convective mass flux and anvil outflow, and their relationships to various environments, within the tropics.
